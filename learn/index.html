    Chapter 0

    Thinking Like the Machine

    A hands-on course in the skills a computer science degree drills without always naming them: keeping an accurate picture of what the computer does, debugging by testing "what if" worlds, and picking the data shape that makes a problem easy.

    The argument you read

    Joe Marshall's blog post Not Everyone Can Code says debugging needs one particular thinking skill: counterfactual reasoning. That means reasoning about something you believe is false, the way "What if the dinosaurs hadn't died out?" asks about a world that didn't happen.

    In debugging it sounds like this: "Suppose this variable is empty, even though I checked it two lines ago. How could that happen?" Your instinct says it can't be empty, because you wrote the check. To find the bug you have to set that instinct aside and take the "impossible" seriously.

    He borrows a three-step ladder from Judea Pearl, a computer scientist who studies cause and effect. Each step asks a harder kind of question:

    StepThe questionWhat it sounds like when debugging
    1. Noticing a patternWhat tends to happen together?"The total is wrong whenever the cart has a mug in it."
    2. Trying a changeWhat happens if I change something?"If I remove the mug, the total is right again."
    3. Explaining the causeWhy did it happen?"For the total to be NaN (JavaScript's 'not a number' result), some multiplication must have been given something that isn't a number. Which one, and how did it get there?"

    Steps 1 and 2 can be done by trial and error. Step 3 needs a picture of what the code does, line by line, so you can work backwards from the symptom to the cause.

    His argument is that many people who write code stay on steps 1 and 2. They reuse patterns that worked before, combine libraries (ready-made code written by other people), or ask an AI assistant, without picturing what the computer actually does. That works until something breaks in a new way. Then only step 3 helps.

    He adds that concurrency makes step 3 much harder. Concurrency means several tasks running at overlapping times, such as two network requests, or two people saving the same document. The bug can depend on the exact order the tasks' steps happened in. There are many possible orders, and testing will show you only a few of them. Chapter 7 lets you try this yourself.

    What the research adds

    His description of the skill is useful. His conclusion, that some people simply can't learn it, is less well supported. Here's what studies of how people learn programming say:

    • The studies he cites tested people who had never been taught. The 1970s research he mentions (by Dulit, Keating and Neimark) measured abstract "what if" reasoning, in the style of the psychologist Jean Piaget, in people with no training in it. That shows where people start, not how far they can get with practice. (I couldn't check the exact percentages he quotes.)
    • Debugging gets better when it's taught as a step-by-step method. In one experiment, students taught a systematic debugging procedure became better at finding bugs, and more confident, than a similar group who weren't taught it (Michaeli & Romeike, 2019). A 2021 study found that few beginners test their guesses methodically on their own. That points to a skill nobody teaches, rather than one people can't learn.
    • Knowing what each step is for helps. When the worked examples in an introductory programming course labelled every step with its purpose, fewer students failed or dropped out (Margulieux, Morrison & Decker, 2020). This course explains the purpose of each step for the same reason.
    • Beginners need an accurate picture of what the computer does. Many beginner mistakes come from a wrong idea of what happens when a line of code runs. Researchers call the simplified but correct picture a "notional machine" (du Boulay; Sorva, 2013). Chapter 1 builds one with you.
    • Reading code and writing code are separate skills, and reading comes first. A teaching method called PRIMM has learners Predict what code does, Run it, Investigate it, Modify it, and then Make their own (Sentance and colleagues). "Parsons problems" give you the right lines of code in the wrong order to rearrange, so you can practise how a program is built without also having to remember every symbol (Ericson and colleagues). This course uses both.

    The idea this course is built on. Marshall himself says few people can reason about concurrency "without the aid of formal models or pure architectural restraint". In plainer words: without written-down tools, or without designing the program so the dangerous cases can't happen. Experienced programmers don't hold every "what if" in their heads either. They move each one out of their head into something they can look at:

    • a printed value (Chapter 2)
    • a line in the code that stops the program when an assumption turns out wrong (Chapter 6)
    • an automatic test (Chapter 6)
    • a data design where a bad situation can't even be written down (Chapters 3 and 6)

    Those habits can be learned, and teaching them is what this course is for.

    Chapter 1

    The machine in your head

    Debugging is a comparison between what you think the computer did and what it actually did. If your picture of what the computer does is vague, you have nothing to compare against, and you can't ask "how could this variable be empty?" without knowing how a variable gets its value in the first place. So this chapter makes that picture exact.

    Reading JavaScript: the syntax this course uses

    Every program, whatever the language, does the same few jobs: it remembers values, groups them, decides what to do, repeats work, packages steps so they can be reused, and shows results. Each piece of syntax exists for one of those jobs. Learn the job first and the symbols stop being arbitrary.

    Rule 1: a name points at a value

    A variable is a label you stick on a value so you can find it again. Assignment (=) moves the label onto a value. It isn't an equation, and it doesn't tie two labels together for good: b = a means "put label b on whatever a is on right now".

    What happens after that depends on which of two kinds of value the label is on:

    Plain valuesContainers
    Which onesNumbers, strings (text), booleans, undefined, nullArrays [ ] and objects { }
    Can the value itself change?No. Nothing can turn the 5 itself into a 6. x = x + 1 works out a new value, 6, and moves the label onto it. String operations work the same way: name.toUpperCase() gives you a new string and leaves the old one as it was.Yes. list.push(3) adds to that same array. user.name = "Grace" edits that same object.
    What b = a gives youIn practice, a separate copy. Because nothing can change the value, it makes no difference whether a and b share it.Two labels on one container. A change made through either name shows up through both.
    Everyday comparisonA price written on a sticky note. Copy it onto a second note and the two are independent.A shared Google Doc. b = a sends someone the link, not a copy of the file. If they edit it, you see the edit.
    To get an independent copyNothing to do.[...list] or { ...obj }, or structuredClone(obj) when there are containers inside the container.

    Why it works this way. Containers can be big (a cart, a list of 10,000 products) and get handed around constantly: into functions, between parts of a page. Copying the whole thing at every step would be slow, and usually you want every part of the program to see the same cart. So JavaScript hands over the link and leaves copying to you. Python, Java, C# and most other languages behave the same way.

    Why you need to know it. Three bugs that come straight from this rule:

    • You write applyDiscount(cart), which lowers each item's price, to show a preview. The real cart on the page changes too, because the function received a link to the cart, not a copy of it.
    • In React, cart.push(item); setCart(cart); doesn't update the screen. React checks whether it got a different container, and it's the same one. setCart([...cart, item]) makes a new container, so the screen updates.
    • You write const mySettings = defaultSettings; and change mySettings.theme. Now the defaults have changed for everyone who uses them.

    The stepper below shows this happening line by line. The left column lists each name and what it's on. Containers appear in the right column with a number such as #1, and a name that holds the link to one shows → #1. (Programmers call that right-hand area "the heap". You don't need the word, but you'll see it in documentation. It's unrelated to the "heap" data structure in Chapter 4.) Before each click, say out loud what you expect to change.

    Rule 2: each function call gets a fresh set of names

    When a function is called, that call gets its own private set of names. Programmers call it a frame. The function's inputs (its parameters) are set to the values you passed in, and any variable created inside the function lives only in that private set. When the function returns, the set is thrown away.

    Why you need to know it. It's what lets you reuse names safely: two functions can each have a variable called total without interfering. It explains a common error message: use a variable outside the function that created it and you get ReferenceError: tax is not defined. It also explains the applyDiscount bug above. The parameter is a new name, but it holds a link to the same container you passed in.

    Beliefs that break programs

    These are the most common wrong beliefs researchers find in people learning to program. Each one causes bugs that are very hard to find, because when you run the code in your head using the wrong belief, you get the same wrong answer the computer did, so nothing looks wrong.

    Wrong beliefWhat the machine actually does
    b = a links b and a foreverFor plain values, b effectively gets its own copy at that moment. For containers, both names point at the same one until one of them is pointed somewhere else.
    const makes an object unchangeableconst stops the name from pointing elsewhere. The object itself can still change.
    {...obj} makes a full copyIt copies only the outer container. Any containers inside it (an array of tags, say) are shared between the original and the copy, as the next example shows. structuredClone(obj) copies everything, all the way down.
    Code runs in the order it's writtenWithin one function, yes. But code you hand over to run later doesn't. That includes a function that runs when someone clicks a button, code after an await (which waits for something like a server reply), and timers. That code runs when its moment comes, possibly after lines written below it. Chapter 7 is about this.
    If the user typed a number, I have a numberData from a form, a file, the address bar or a server arrives as text until you convert it, even when it looks like a number. So "2" + 2 joins the text and gives "22". Convert with Number(value) first.
    If the program didn't crash, the value is fineJavaScript usually carries on with undefined ("no value") or NaN ("not a number") instead of stopping. The wrong value travels through the program, and if anything crashes, it's far from where the mistake happened.

    Habit to build: when you read code you didn't write, keep a scratch "names and containers" table on paper for the variables that matter. It feels slow for a week, then it becomes automatic.     Chapter 2

    Debugging as a procedure

    Marshall's hard part is taking seriously an explanation you're sure is false, like "this value is empty even though I checked it". It gets much easier once that explanation is just one line on a written list. You don't have to believe it. You write it down as one possible explanation, then run a quick check to rule it in or out.

    This chapter calls each possible explanation a world: one version of events that would produce the bug you're seeing. Listing worlds and checking them one at a time turns the hard question "why did this happen?" (step 3 of the ladder in Chapter 0) into a series of simple "what do I see if I look here?" checks (step 2). Anyone can run those.

    The method, step by step

    1. Observe. Write down the symptom exactly: what you did, what you expected, what you got. Then find a way to make it happen again whenever you want. A bug you can't trigger on purpose is one you can't check a fix against.
    2. List the worlds. Ask: "For this result to appear, what would have to be true?" Write down every answer, including the ones you're sure are false. Especially those: the bug is hiding in something you believe can't happen, or you'd have found it already.
    3. Predict. For each world, ask: "If this were the real one, what would I see if I looked at X?" A guess that doesn't predict anything you can look at can't be checked, so rewrite it until it does.
    4. Experiment. Pick the check that rules out the most worlds for the least effort. Typical checks: print a value with console.log, try a smaller input, or temporarily switch a line off by putting // in front of it.
    5. Conclude and record. Cross out the worlds the evidence ruled out. Keep a logbook (a running note) of what you tried, what you saw and what it means. Repeat until one world is left.

    Andreas Zeller, a researcher who studies debugging, calls this scientific debugging in his book Why Programs Fail, because it's the scientific method applied to code. The logbook matters more than it seems. Without one, you forget what you already ruled out and end up checking the same things in circles.

    Search, don't stare: the wolf fence
    
  
    Often a value starts out right and comes out wrong after passing through many steps. Reading every step looking for the mistake is slow. Instead, check the value halfway through. If it's already wrong there, the bug is in the first half; if it's still right, it's in the second half. Each check rules out half of the remaining steps, so eight steps need at most three checks, and a thousand need about ten.
    
  
    The name comes from an old joke: to find a wolf in Alaska, build a fence down the middle, listen for which side it howls on, and repeat on that side. If you use Git (a tool that saves every version of your code), the command git bisect does the same halving across your saved versions to find the one where something broke.

    Chapter 3
    
  
    Data shapes come first
    
  
    The bug lab ended in a data problem: one item had quantity where the code expected qty. That's typical. By a data's shape we mean how it's organised: a list, a single record with named parts, a lookup table and so on. A lot of confusing code is confusing because its data has the wrong shape, and the code is twisting itself to work around that. Choose the right shape and the code tends to become simple.
    
  
    "Show me your flowchart and conceal your tables, and I shall continue to be mystified. Show me your tables, and I won't usually need your flowchart; it'll be obvious."Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month (1975). Brooks managed some of the first very large software projects. By "flowchart" he meant the steps of the program, and by "tables" its data.
    
  
    "Data dominates. If you've chosen the right data structures and organized things well, the algorithms will almost always be self-evident."Rob Pike, Notes on Programming in C (1989). Pike later co-created the Go programming language. "Data structures" means the shapes in this chapter; "algorithms" means the step-by-step procedures that work on them.
    
  
    The textbook How to Design Programs turns this into a rule: write down what your data looks like before writing any function, because the shape of the data decides the shape of the code. A list of items leads to a loop over them. Data nested inside data of the same kind (comments with replies) leads to a function that calls itself, which Chapter 6 covers. Data that can be one of several kinds leads to an if with one branch per kind.

    Tuples, records and the key trap
    
  
    A tuple is a short group of values with a fixed length, where each position has a meaning: (x, y) on a chart, or (latitude, longitude) on a map. Python has a built-in tuple type. JavaScript doesn't, so people use a short array instead, like [x, y]. You'll see this in React: const [count, setCount] = useState(0) gives you back two things, the current value and a function to change it, and the square brackets on the left unpack them into two names.
    
  
    That's fine for two values whose meaning is obvious. Beyond two or three, or whenever the order could get mixed up, use an object with named fields instead. Mapping tools are a real example: Leaflet (a popular map library) expects [lat, lng], while GeoJSON (a common file format for map data) uses [lng, lat]. Mixing them up has put plenty of map pins in the middle of the ocean. { lat, lng } can't be swapped by accident.
    
  
    A Python tuple can't be changed after it's created, so Python lets you use one as a dictionary key: visited[(2, 3)] = True works. JavaScript is different. A Map treats two arrays as the same key only if they are the very same container (Chapter 1, Rule 1), not if they merely contain the same values. Predict this one carefully:
    
  
    
  
    When should you use an object, and when a Map? Use an object for a record: one thing whose fields you know when writing the code, like a user's name and email. Use a Map for a collection: many entries looked up by keys you only find out while the program runs, like user IDs. One reason: an object turns every key into text, so two different keys can silently become one:
    
  
    

  
    Real data nests
    
  
    Real programs combine the shapes: a Map from ID to a record, where each record holds an array, and so on. One combination you'll use often is grouping: a Map from a key (a city) to an array of everything with that key (the people who live there). It's how you build "orders per customer" or "products per category".
    
  
    
  
    When the same thing appears in several places, like a user who wrote several posts and comments, store it once (in a Map by ID) and refer to it by its ID everywhere else. This is called normalizing, and it's how databases are designed. The reason: if the user's name is copied into every post, renaming the user means finding and updating every copy, and if you miss one, the copies disagree.

Use the arrows to put them in order, then check. The indentation (how far each line is pushed right) is a clue: lines inside a block are indented further than the line that opens it.

      Chapter 6
      
  
      Techniques: when to use what
      
  
      When something goes wrong, the fewer possible explanations (the "worlds" from Chapter 2) there are, the faster you find the cause. Each technique below removes some of them. For each one, this chapter says what it is, when to use it, and which kind of bug it prevents.
      

  
      Loops and map / filter / reduce
      
  
      Most loops over an array do one of a few common jobs. Arrays have a built-in function for each job. Using the right one tells the reader at a glance what the loop is for ("keep only the paid orders"), and leaves less room for mistakes like forgetting to add to the result.
      
  
      
    
      
      
      You want to…Use
      
      
        
      Turn each item into one new itemmap
      
        
      Keep some itemsfilter
      
        
      Combine everything into one value (sum, max, object)reduce, or a plain loop if reduce gets hard to read
      
        
      Find the first match, or ask "any?" / "all?"find, some, every; they stop early
      
        
      Stop partway, track several things at once, or squeeze out speedA plain for…of loop. It's more flexible, and a fine choice whenever the built-in functions make the code harder to read.
      
            
    
      
  
      
  
      

  
      Lookup table instead of an if/else chain
      
  
      Sometimes code is a long chain like if (country === "DE") rate = 4.99; else if (country === "FR") rate = 5.99; …, where each branch just turns one value into another. That's really a table written as code. Put the table in an object or Map instead. Adding a country becomes a one-line change to the data, and there's exactly one place that decides what happens for a country that isn't listed, instead of a chain that quietly does nothing.
      
  
      

  
      Guard clauses
      
  
      A guard clause is an if at the top of a function that checks for a problem and returns straight away if it finds one. Put all of these first, then write the normal case below them. The alternative is ifs nested inside ifs, where it's hard to see which condition you're inside. With guard clauses, each problem is dealt with in one line, and below them you can stop thinking about it.
      
  
      

  
      Pure functions and side effects
      
  
      A function is pure when two things are true. Its answer depends only on the inputs you pass in. And it doesn't change anything outside itself, which includes variables elsewhere, containers it was handed, the page and files. Any such outside change is called a side effect.
      
  
      Why it matters: a pure function is the easiest code to debug. Call it with the input that failed and you've reproduced the bug, every time. A function that reads a variable from somewhere else has a hidden input: its answer can change because of code far away, as the example shows. Programs do need side effects (saving, loading, updating the page), so the advice is to keep your calculations pure and keep the side effects in a thin outer layer that calls them. Some people call this "functional core, imperative shell".
      
  
      

  
      Make impossible states impossible
      
  
      Picture a search page that tracks what's happening with three true/false variables: isLoading, hasError and hasData. Three true/false values give 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 combinations, but only four of them describe a real situation. The other four are nonsense, like "loading and failed at the same time". Nothing in the code stops them from happening, so one day some sequence of clicks will produce one and the page will show something strange. Tick the boxes and see.
      
  
      
    
      
      
      
        
      Three booleans
      
        
      
          
          
          
        
      
        
      
      
      
      
      
        
      What the screen shows
      
        
      
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
    
      
  
      
  
      The fix is to replace the three variables with one status field that can hold only the four real situations. Any extra data belongs to its situation: the error message lives inside the error state, so you can't end up with an error message and no error.
      
  
      // 4 states, all of them valid
{ status: "idle" }
{ status: "loading" }
{ status: "error",   message: "Network down" }
{ status: "success", data: [/* … */] }

switch (state.status) {
  case "idle":    return showSearchBox();
  case "loading": return showSpinner();
  case "error":   return showError(state.message);
  case "success": return showResults(state.data);
}
      
  
      This is a small state machine: a fixed list of named situations (states), where the thing is always in exactly one of them. Use one whenever you catch yourself adding another true/false variable to describe what stage something is at. Forms, file uploads, checkouts, network connections and game rounds are typical cases.
      

  
      Assertions and checking at the boundary
      
  
      An assertion is a line that checks something you believe is always true and stops the program with a clear message if it isn't. It's a "what if this weren't true?" written into the code. Without it, a bad value travels on and causes trouble somewhere else (like the NaN in the bug lab). With it, the program stops at the exact line where your assumption broke and says which one.
      
  
      
  
      The best place for these checks is the boundary: the point where data enters your program, such as a form, a reply from a server, or a file. Check and convert the data there, once, into exactly the shape the rest of your code expects. After that point, the rest of your program can trust its data instead of every function checking again. The bug lab's quantity/qty mix-up would have been caught here. (If you later use TypeScript, a version of JavaScript that checks types, libraries such as Zod do this checking and converting for you.)
      
  
      

  
      Recursion for data that contains itself
      
  
      A recursive function is one that calls itself. That sounds circular, but it fits data that contains smaller copies of itself, called a tree: folders inside folders, comments with replies to replies, menus with submenus. The function handles one item, then calls itself on each item inside it, and stops when an item has nothing inside. A loop can't easily do this, because you don't know in advance how deep the nesting goes. For a flat list, use a normal loop.
      
  
      

  
      Tests: counterfactuals you keep
      
  
      A test is a small piece of code that calls your function with a chosen input and checks the answer, for example "cartTotal of an empty cart should be 0". You write it once and re-run it after every change, so a bug you fixed can't quietly come back. It's a "what if" you write down once and the computer checks forever. When choosing what to test, go through this list. Each line is a situation your code will meet sooner or later:
      
  
        
    
      • Empty: no items, empty string, zero
        • 
    
      • One: a single item. This catches "off-by-one" mistakes, where a loop runs one time too many or too few.
        • 
    
      • Many and duplicates: the same item twice
        • 
    
      • Missing or misspelled fields: quantity instead of qty
        • 
    
      • Wrong type: "8" instead of 8, null
        • 
    
      • Boundaries: exactly 3 items when the discount starts at 3; negative numbers; very large numbers
        • 
    
      • Text: spaces at the ends, capital letters, accents and emoji. A classic: "İzmir".toLowerCase() doesn't give you "izmir"; the dot on the capital İ becomes an extra invisible character.
        • 
  
      

  
      Architecture in five decisions
      
  
      "Architecture" just means how the parts of a program are organised and connected. These five decisions come up in every project.
      
  
      
    
      
      
      DecisionRule of thumb, and why
      
      
        
      Where does each piece of state live? (State is any data that changes while the app runs, like the cart contents or the logged-in user.)Give it one owner: one place in the code that holds it and changes it. Everything else asks that owner or gets a copy. If two parts of the page each keep their own "cart", sooner or later they'll disagree.
      
        
      Should I make this code reusable?Write it plainly the first time. Copy it the second time. The third time, turn it into a shared function, because by then you can see which parts really change between uses. Making things reusable too early usually guesses wrong.
      
        
      Class, or plain objects and functions?A class is a template for objects that come with their own built-in functions. Default to plain objects and separate functions. Use a class when an object has a rule it must always protect, like a bank account whose balance must never go negative: the class can make sure every change goes through a check.
      
        
      Inheritance or composition?Inheritance means one class takes over everything from another and adds to it (a Manager is an Employee plus extras). Composition means building something from smaller parts you plug in. Prefer composition. With long inheritance chains it gets hard to tell which version of a function will actually run.
      
        
      How do I split code into files?Keep together the code that changes together. Three things change for different reasons: what's shown on screen (the UI), your app's rules ("free shipping over €50"), and the code that loads and saves data. Keep them apart, so changing one doesn't break the others.
      
            
    
      
  
      
  

      



      
  
      Chapter 7
      
  
      Concurrency: debugging many histories
      
  
      Concurrency means several tasks in progress at the same time: two requests to a server, two browser tabs saving the same document, two customers buying the last item in stock. Each task runs in small steps, and the steps of different tasks can interleave in many possible orders.
      
  
      Marshall calls this "counterfactual reasoning on steroids". With one task there's one order of events to think through. With several, there are many, and the bug may only appear in an order you've never seen during testing.
      
  
      JavaScript in a browser runs one piece of code at a time, but it isn't safe from this. Whenever your code waits, for a server reply (await), a timer, or a click, other code gets to run in the meantime and can change things before yours carries on.
      

  
      The lost update
      
  
      Imagine two deposits of €10 into a bank account holding €100, arriving at the same moment from two devices. Each deposit is handled by its own task in three steps: read the balance into a private copy (local), add 10 to the copy, then write the copy back as the new balance. Both should finish with €120. Click the buttons to choose which task takes its next step. Can you get €120? Can you get something else?

      Two tasks of three steps each can interleave in twenty possible orders, and only two of them give the right answer. A test usually runs one task completely and then the other, which is one of the two lucky orders, so the test passes and the bug ships. Nobody checks all the orders in their head. What works is the "architectural restraint" from the article: design the program so the dangerous orders can't happen. The checkbox above shows the simplest version.
      

  
      The same bug in a search box
      
  
      Many search boxes ask the server for results as you type. A user types "ca", then "cat", so two requests go out. The server happens to answer the first one slowly and the second one quickly, so the answers arrive in the opposite order to the typing. The example below fakes that delay with a timer.
      
  
      
  
      The fix: number each request, remember the newest number, and ignore any answer that doesn't belong to the newest request. (Browsers also have a built-in tool, AbortController, that cancels the old request entirely.)
      
  
      

  
      Designs that rule out the dangerous orders
      
  
      Every bug in this chapter comes from the same thing: two tasks changing the same data with a gap between reading it and writing it. Each design below closes the gap or removes the sharing.
      
  
      
    
      
      
      DesignHow it helps, and where you'll meet it
      
      
        
      Don't share data that changesGive each piece of changing data one owner. Other tasks ask the owner to make the change instead of changing it themselves, so all changes happen in one place, one at a time.
      
        
      Data that can't be changedIf nothing can change a value after it's created, and you make new copies instead (as with [...cart, item] in Chapter 1), no task can ever see it half-changed. This is the reason React wants new copies.
      
        
      QueuesTasks put requests into a queue, and a single worker handles them in order, one at a time. Many tasks can add requests; only one acts on them. Email sending and payment processing usually work this way.
      
        
      Do read-change-write in one stepLet something that can't be interrupted do all three steps at once. Databases can: in SQL, the language most databases use, UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + 10 does the whole deposit in one step, instead of your code reading the balance and writing it back later. Programmers call one-step operations atomic, from the Greek for "can't be cut".
      
        
      LocksA lock lets only one task at a time into a section of code; the others wait their turn. It works, but it's easy to get wrong. The classic failure is a deadlock: two tasks each waiting for a lock the other holds, forever. Prefer the designs above when you can.
      
        
      Make operations safe to repeatNetworks fail, and apps often re-send a request that seemed to fail but actually got through. If the operation is "set this order's status to paid", doing it twice is harmless. If it's "add a payment of €50", doing it twice charges the customer twice. The technical term is idempotent.
